## 🔬 Deep Dive: Fiber Behaviors on Knitting Machines

### Merino Wool – Tension Sensitive
When clients request buttery next-to-skin comfort on 18-gauge knits, merino is almost always the solution. Finer, smoother cuticles mean far less itch compared to conventional wool.

Working it on fine gauge machines brings real challenges though. 18G merino is notoriously sensitive to tension shifts. I’ve stood beside running flat machines where tension drifted merely ±2 grams, and watched panels turn into uneven, unusable messes. 

> ⚠️ **Engineering Pro-Tip:** Before confirming POs for fine gauge merino, ask to view live tension monitoring footage. If your manufacturer hasn’t invested in digital constant-tension yarn feeders, prepare for excessive waste and delays. Don’t settle only for lab dip submissions.

### Standard Wool – Wash Stability Risks
Think chunky Aran knits, heavy gauge outerwear — standard wool delivers rigid structure. Its thicker, stiffer fibres are a strength for oversized, robust silhouettes, yet a poor choice for slim-fit roll necks.

Wash stability remains the biggest headache. A 3G wool knit can shift half a size up or down purely from slightly extended steam finishing cycles. 

> 💡 **Cost & Spec Tip:** If you need improved dimensional stability while controlling costs, blending with acrylic makes practical sense. An 80/20 wool-acrylic mix remains an enduring reliable formula.

### Mohair – Brushing Mishaps
Mohair originates from Angora goats; its fibre lacks meaningful surface scales. This characteristic creates that signature shine and airy fluff, yet it also comes with barely any inherent elasticity.

On knitting equipment, mohair cannot hold stitch shape independently. You will need a nylon core blend at often higher percentages to maintain structure. 

> ⚠️ **Quality Control Red Flag:** Many overlook post-knitting brushing. Inexperienced operators over-brush panels and burn holes straight through the body; under-brush, and the knit appears flat. Enquire how many brushing test runs they complete per order. Evasive responses are a red flag.

### Baby Alpaca – Low Shape Recovery
Let’s clear up a misunderstanding: “baby” alpaca describes the first shearing harvested from mature alpacas, delivering the softest fibre grade. Hollow fibre construction creates remarkable warmth without excess weight, and zero lanolin delivers natural hypoallergenic performance. 

**The downside:** Extremely low shape recovery. Hang heavy-gauge baby alpaca knits on standard hangers, and gravitational stretch will warp lengths rapidly. 

> 🔧 **Construction Fix:** Shoulder and back neck seams require **stay tape reinforcement** as standard. Opt for 7-gauge structures to add minimal rigidity, and always ship folded.

### Cotton – The Transitional Workhorse
Cotton is unfairly written off as merely summer tee material. High-twist cotton yarn on 12-gauge knits produces excellent trans-seasonal pieces. It feels smooth against skin, avoids static build-up, and accepts dyes beautifully.

The main drawback is visible bagging around elbows and cuffs over wear. 

> 💡 **Blend Suggestion:** A simple workaround is small cashmere additions (e.g., 95/5 cotton-cashmere). You retain cotton’s cool hand feel alongside subtle elasticity to resist stretching.

## 🎯 Fibre Selection Matrix for Brands

For anyone building collection roadmaps focused on balanced return rates:

*   **Premium Launch Styles (Hypoallergenic selling story):** Baby Alpaca in 5GG or 7GG. Let natural texture take centre stage.
*   **Core Tailored Knits (Direct skin contact):** 14GG or 18GG Merino. Confirm your factory has matching precision machinery.
*   **Bold Statement Silhouettes (Airy fuzzy effects):** Loose 3GG Mohair. Never cut corners on brushing QC.
*   **Large Volume Mass Market (Durability & margins):** Standard wool blends or cotton-heavy mixes. Consistently commercially viable.
